Understanding Internet Connection Types

Not all internet is created equal. Here's what you need to know about each technology.

Fiber Optic

Fiber Up to 5 Gbps

The gold standard. Fiber uses light pulses through glass cables for symmetrical upload and download speeds, extremely low latency, and exceptional reliability.

Fastest speeds available
Symmetrical upload/download
Ultra-low latency
Not affected by weather
Limited availability
Higher installation cost

Cable

Cable Up to 1.2 Gbps

Uses the same coaxial cables as cable TV. Widely available with fast download speeds, though upload speeds are typically much slower.

Widely available
Fast download speeds
Bundling options with TV
Slower upload speeds
Peak-hour congestion
Shared bandwidth

DSL

DSL Up to 100 Mbps

Uses existing telephone lines. Available almost everywhere but speeds decrease the farther you are from the provider's central office.

Very widely available
Dedicated line (no sharing)
Often the cheapest option
Slowest technology

5G / Fixed Wireless

5G Up to 1 Gbps

Uses cellular towers to deliver home internet wirelessly. No cables needed — just plug in the gateway. Fast-expanding coverage with competitive speeds.

Growing coverage

7 Ways to Save on Your Internet Bill

The average American overpays by $10–20/month. Here's how to keep more money in your pocket.

Call your provider's retention department and mention you're considering switching. Many will offer promotional pricing, credits, or speed upgrades. Be polite but firm, and have competitor pricing ready.

Most providers charge $10–15/month to rent a router. A quality router costs $80–150 and pays for itself in under a year. Check your provider's compatible equipment list before purchasing.

Many people pay for gigabit when 200 Mbps would suffice. Use our speed test to check what you actually use during peak hours. Downgrading from 1 Gig to 300 Mbps can save $20–40/month.

If you've been with the same provider for years, you're likely paying more than new customers. Consider switching when your contract ends to take advantage of introductory rates.

Triple-play bundles often seem like a deal but include services you don't use. Internet-only plans are almost always cheaper if you rely on streaming services instead of cable TV.

The FCC's Affordable Connectivity Program provides up to $30/month toward internet bills for qualifying households. Many providers also have their own low-income plans offering service for $20–30/month.

Many providers offer $5–10/month off when you enroll in autopay and paperless billing. It's free money for something you'd probably do anyway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the most common internet questions

Download speed measures how fast data comes TO your device (streaming, browsing). Upload speed measures how fast data goes FROM your device (video calls, cloud backups). Most people need fast download speeds, but remote workers should also prioritize upload.

Latency (milliseconds) is the time for data to travel from your device to a server and back. Under 20ms is excellent, 20–50ms is good, over 100ms causes lag. Fiber has the lowest latency, satellite the highest.

A data cap limits total data per month. Many "unlimited" plans have soft caps (e.g., 1.2 TB) after which speeds may be throttled. Fiber plans typically have no caps. 1 TB is enough for most households.

Contracts lock in lower prices for 12–24 months but have termination fees ($100–400). Month-to-month costs more but offers flexibility. If staying 1–2+ years, a contract is usually the better deal.

Providers advertise "up to" speeds. Common causes: Wi-Fi interference (try 5GHz), outdated router, too many devices, peak-hour congestion. If consistently below 70% of advertised, contact your provider.

1) Research providers in your area. 2) Schedule installation with the new provider. 3) Keep old service active until new one works. 4) Cancel old service and return equipment. 5) Schedule the switch at end of billing cycle.
